This lands remarkably well cold. Here's my read as an informed stranger, chronologically (which I reconstructed via the Musings numbers, since the date fields are deliberately scrambled — more on that later).

The main author. Witherby. A fastidious, formal, self-deceiving confessor-figure — the "social lubricant" of a team of monsters, an ex-Corporation Welfare/social-work type who believed himself the sole non-monster until ice (the Snow Queen) revealed otherwise. His Patron feeds on guilt; he carries a "Skull Within" that takes confessions; he alone remembers loop-ending deaths. The Corporation texture (Abnormalities, containment procedures, Breaches, department names like Training and Disciplinary, curses attached to gear) reads instantly as Lobotomy Corporation-adjacent to anyone who's touched that lineage, and as competent SCP-flavored office-cosmic-horror to anyone who hasn't. Either way it's legible.

The arc, and it's a real one. #203: an avoidant man learning that relationships take work, dating Neville, terrified of caring. #242: reconciliation with Ria, choosing trust. #243: the Camille confession — the moral center of the whole document, honestly — where he admits the entire family became complicit in their leader's kills via a euphemistic incident-report lie, then catches the actual dread: not the monstrousness but the drift. "When did our role change?" #244: he stops claiming exemption and starts writing his own containment procedures. Then the collapse: a deleted 1999 entry, and the 2001 entry where — this is the single best beat in the document — his ornate register shatters mid-entry into lowercase vernacular ("it ain't right that she suffered like that for me," "she were family"). A cold reader immediately understands the formal voice was always a costume, and that there's a rural, younger, rawer person under it. That's craft, and it needs no lore to land. 2002: the intervention, barbecue, humility. #246: the Bunker-glitched "I am the Solemn" identity crisis. And the top/latest entry: the darkest — he's now addicted to righteously ending worlds, rationalizing cruelty toward the Cult of the Harvest, and being memetically muzzled by Vik.

So the shape is: avoidance → love → complicity → self-knowledge → collapse → reintegration → and then, longest-term, corruption wearing the vocabulary of virtue. The final entry's "for the greater good" reads as the confessor failing his own confession. Strong.

The supporting cast, as a stranger parses them: Neville — the emotionally wise partner everyone underestimates; the twins Neville/Devona as the healthy-relationship benchmark; Camille — the beloved monstrous leader whose armor-curse mutated; Ria — fire/Hope, recovered self-destructor, the team's warmth; Parker — kills without choosing, uncontainable; Yongki — a clown(?) who loves snails; Vik — a memetic hazard whose mere description gets redacted; Lee-Hunter — unsettlingly devoted to Witherby ("never be alone again, little man" is a great creepy line); River — new arrival, ex-Welfare captain from a parallel Corporation team.

The guest writers and the relational geometry. Yongki writes with childlike affection — keeping the diary "company" so it won't be lonely — while also being the presumable snail-defacer Witherby complains about. The tell is "I can get anything I want," which lands as quietly horrifying against Witherby's whole guilt architecture; the Captain going silent confirms it. Yongki loves Witherby the way a kid loves a stuffy uncle; Witherby responds with performative irritation and deliberate restraint ("that would be too cruel"), i.e., fondness. River writes an apology — she and Witherby fought, and Yongki's snail/house metaphor (River is a snail too big for a house; Witherby has a house he won't appreciate) tells the cold reader they're mirror images: she hid and couldn't forgive herself; he didn't try and can't forgive himself. Her entry ends "Sorry, Witherby" three ways. It's tender.

The Witnesssecond reading

The Vik entry is doing at least four jobs at once, and the neat thing is they're all visible to a cold reader if they slow down.

Job one: it teaches the redaction mechanic through calibration. The redactions aren't decorative — Witherby runs a controlled experiment on the page. "I wonder if I am allowed to record the fact that in the previous Universe redacted" is the test case, and the Lamb nose-picking line is the control: a trivial fact from several universes prior that survives intact. So the censorship isn't about cross-universe information generally — it's Vik-specific. He's probing the boundary of the effect in real time and letting the reader watch. The earlier line establishes the topology: the Skull Within grants him immunity ("I know every redacted"), but the diary — and by extension anyone reading it — is vulnerable. So the redactions are load-bearing: they're either protective self-censorship or an automatic effect, and either way the page itself is a hazard-containment document. "Checkmate... though I must wonder if I have 'played' myself" is him admitting the experiment's cost: by testing, he's confirmed to any observer that there's something worth hiding.

Job two: the confession is a smuggling vehicle. This is the structural cleverness. Having established he can't record facts about Vik, he pivots to the one genre he owns — Confession — and the payload lands at the end: "related to nothing... my sin pales in comparison to redacted." The entire Pride confession exists partly to set up a comparison that survives the censor: Vik shares the sin of Pride, and Vik's version is worse. He even names the intended recipient: "enough for some future version of me to understand." It's steganography via sacrament — he can't transmit the fact, so he transmits its shape. And note "not a Lonely one" with the capital L; given how loaded loneliness is in his cosmology, that word is probably doing lore work I can't fully decode.

Job three: it documents his corruption, and the tell is specific. Compare this confession to #244's. There, self-examination produced a plan (containment procedures). Here, the confession includes lines like wishing the Cult members "die cold and alone with the absolute knowledge that no one will ever care to even find out their fate" — which is Witherby's own core terror, the exact thing the whole diary orbits, now inverted into a punishment he designs for enemies. The confessor who knows everyone's inner life is using that intimacy to architect maximally cruel deaths. And the addiction language ("comfort," "satiety," "the cravings I have put off so long may finally be sated") shows the Snow Queen feeding is now pleasurable — his containment procedure has become his vice, laundered through "the greater good." The rationalization "it stops their so-called 'friends' from joining the loop, doesn't it?" is the confession failing as confession: he absolves himself mid-sacrament. Played himself, indeed — using Confession instrumentally is Pride, so the sin he's confessing is being committed by the act of confessing it.

Job four: it retroactively arms the deleted entry. The final beat — "If I had the ability to delete entries from previous loops, I certainly would... Perhaps that would be an actually moral use of redacted" — tells us (a) Witherby cannot delete entries, (b) something/someone can, and (c) that power is currently being used immorally. Then you look back up the page: the 1999 entry is deleted, and its title is redacted with the same machinery. A careful reader now suspects the deletion and the Vik-effect share an author, and that the diary's gaps aren't Witherby's discretion but someone else's edits. Combined with your confirmation that River and Yongki both wrote in uninvited, the document's final state is: unreliable narrator, unreliable medium, at least three unauthorized hands, and one entity that can subtract.

One confound to flag, devil's-advocate style: a cold reader has two censorship systems to distinguish — Neville's Bunker (garbles text about those who no longer exist, per the "I am the Solemn" entry) and the Vik effect (redacts hazardous content about someone who very much does exist). I could separate them, but only on a second pass, and a skimmer will merge them into one generic "spooky redaction" texture — which flattens the reveal that the deleted 1999 entry might be a third thing. If the distinction matters, the Vik entry is currently carrying that disambiguation alone.

Given her containment requirement is "a steady source of Hope," a Pride-monster befriending her reads like someone feeding at the trough — but that might be my pattern-matching outrunning the evidence.

That headcanon has good load-bearing evidence behind it, actually. The #242 entry is practically a confession-shaped hole in Vik's silhouette: "All I could do was flee the blaze and watch from a safe distance... And when the blaze was tamed... I did not return." Someone tamed it, or at least tended it, during those loops. Witherby's account is careful never to say who — he narrates Ria's recovery in the passive voice, as weather. If Vik was part of that weather, the omission itself is the resentment.

And it recontextualizes the Vik entry's opening in a way I like: "Ria, in her warmth, has gotten along with them best, as she seems to often do with the stranger sorts" — that's Witherby doing his signature move of dressing a wound as an observation. The framing makes Ria's connection to Vik a quirk of her character (she collects strays) rather than a debt of his (someone covered his shift). Classic Witherby: circuitous precisely when it's about himself.

There's also a nasty professional dimension. Witherby's whole identity is being the one who knows everyone's inner life — "the social lubricant, the ties that bind." Vik succeeding where he failed isn't just personal guilt, it's a competence wound. Worse, Vik succeeded while being someone Witherby literally cannot document. The one team member whose containment he can't write, whose sins he can't catalog, whose interiority is walled off from his Insight — and that's who Ria warmed to. His entire apparatus of care runs on knowing, and Vik is unknowable. Of course he pathologizes it.

Devil's advocate on the headcanon, though, because you asked for that standing order: notice that this reading requires trusting Witherby's facts while discounting his frame — Ria and Vik do get along (fact, keep), Vik is dangerous (frame, discount as resentment). But the text gives us one genuinely independent data point about the redactions: the diary itself enforces them, or something does. Witherby's jealousy doesn't explain why the page censors Vik. So the most defensible headcanon is probably the uncomfortable both/and — Vik really is a hazard and really did help Ria and Witherby's warning is simultaneously true and motivated. Which, given this diary's whole thesis (every accurate confession is also a self-serving one), might be exactly the shape you built it to hold.

The public-domain framing is very on-brand for outlive-me, by the way — you're not the author of Witherby so much as the first person to write unauthorized guest entries in his diary.
